Phase-space action and Hamilton’s equations

Phase-space action (Hamilton’s action functional)

For a system with canonical coordinates \((q_i, p_i)\) and Hamiltonian \(H(q,p,t)\), the phase-space action is

\[ S[q(t),p(t)] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left( \sum_{i=1}^n p_i\,\dot q_i - H(q,p,t) \right)\, dt. \]

The integrand \[ L_{\text{ps}}(q,p,\dot q,t) = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \dot q_i - H(q,p,t) \] is called the phase-space Lagrangian.

Deriving Hamilton’s equations from the phase-space action

We vary both \(q_i(t)\) and \(p_i(t)\), with fixed endpoints: \[ \delta q_i(t_1) = \delta q_i(t_2) = 0 \] The variation of the action is

\[ \delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left[ \sum_i \left( \delta p_i\,\dot q_i + p_i\,\delta\dot q_i \right) - \sum_i\left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\delta q_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\delta p_i \right) \right] dt. \]

Integrate the term with \(p_i\,\delta\dot q_i\) by parts: \[ \int p_i\,\delta\dot q_i\,dt = \left[p_i\,\delta q_i\right]_{t_1}^{t_2} - \int \dot p_i\,\delta q_i\,dt. \] The boundary term vanishes because \(\delta q_i(t_1)=\delta q_i(t_2)=0\). Thus

\[ \delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sum_i \left[ \delta p_i\left(\dot q_i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\right) + \delta q_i\left(-\dot p_i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right) \right] dt. \]

Hamilton’s principle requires \(\delta S = 0\) for all variations \(\delta q_i, \delta p_i\). Since these variations are independent, each coefficient must vanish:

From the \(\delta p_i\) terms: \[ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}. \]

From the \(\delta q_i\) terms: \[ \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}. \]

These are precisely Hamilton’s equations.

Hessian of the phase-space Lagrangian

The phase-space Lagrangian is \[ L_{\text{ps}}(q,p,\dot q,t) = p_i \dot q_i - H(q,p,t). \] It depends linearly on the velocities \(\dot q_i\) and does not depend on \(\dot p_i\). We have \[ \frac{\partial L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial \dot q_i} = p_i, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial \dot q_i \partial \dot q_j} = 0. \] Thus the Hessian with respect to velocities \(\dot q_i\) is identically zero.

If we consider second derivatives with respect to \(q_i\) and \(p_i\), they come only from the Hamiltonian: \[ \frac{\partial^2 L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial q_i \partial q_j} = -\frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q_i \partial q_j},\quad \frac{\partial^2 L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial p_i \partial p_j} = -\frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_i \partial p_j},\quad \frac{\partial^2 L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial q_i \partial p_j} = -\frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q_i \partial p_j}. \] But the velocity block \[ \frac{\partial^2 L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial \dot q_i \partial \dot q_j} = 0 \] is the crucial structural feature.

Why the zero Hessian makes Hamilton’s equations first order

Compare with configuration-space Lagrangian mechanics, where the action \[ S[q] = \int L(q,\dot q,t)\,dt \] leads to Euler–Lagrange equations \[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = 0. \] Expanding the time derivative introduces terms involving \(\ddot q_i\), multiplied by the Hessian \[ W_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \dot q_i \partial \dot q_j}, \] so a nonzero, invertible Hessian gives second-order equations of motion.

In the phase-space case, the Lagrangian \[ L_{\text{ps}} = p_i \dot q_i - H(q,p,t) \] is linear in \(\dot q_i\). Thus \[ \frac{\partial L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial \dot q_i} = p_i \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial \dot q_i}\right) = \dot p_i, \] and no \(\ddot q_i\) terms appear. The Euler–Lagrange equation for \(q_i\) becomes \[ \dot p_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}, \] which is first order in time.

For \(p_i\), since \(L_{\text{ps}}\) does not depend on \(\dot p_i\), \[ \frac{\partial L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial \dot p_i} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial \dot p_i}\right) = 0, \] and the Euler–Lagrange equation reduces to \[ - \frac{\partial L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial p_i} = 0. \] But \[ \frac{\partial L_{\text{ps}}}{\partial p_i} = \dot q_i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \] so \[ \dot q_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \] again a first-order equation.

The vanishing Hessian with respect to velocities in the phase-space Lagrangian guarantees that the resulting equations of motion are first-order in time for both \(q_i\) and \(p_i\). This is the structural reason why Hamilton’s equations are first-order, in contrast to the second-order Euler–Lagrange equations in configuration-space Lagrangian mechanics.
