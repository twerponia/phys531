Chapter 8: Two-Body Central-Force Problems

1. Center of Mass and Relative Coordinates

  • System Setup: For two isolated particles with masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\), position vectors \(\mathbf{r}_1\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2\), interacting via a central conservative force, potential energy depends only on the relative distance \(r = |\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|\)[cite: 2].
  • Relative Position Vector: Defined as \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2\)[cite: 2].
  • Center of Mass (CM) Vector: Defined as \(\mathbf{R} = \frac{m_1\mathbf{r}_1 + m_2\mathbf{r}_2}{M}\), where total mass \(M = m_1 + m_2\)[cite: 2].
  • Reduced Mass (\(\mu\)): Defined as \(\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\)[cite: 2].
  • Kinetic Energy & Lagrangian Separation:
    \[ T = \frac{1}{2}M\dot{\mathbf{R}}^2 + \frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 \] \[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{cm}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{rel}} = \frac{1}{2}M\dot{\mathbf{R}}^2 + \left( \frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 - U(r) \right) \]
    This decouples the system into motion of the CM and relative motion[cite: 2].

2. Equations of Motion in the CM Frame

  • CM Motion: Because \(\mathcal{L}\) is independent of \(\mathbf{R}\), \(\mathbf{R}\) is an ignorable coordinate, yielding \(M\ddot{\mathbf{R}} = 0\) (constant velocity)[cite: 2]. In the CM frame, \(\mathbf{R} = 0\)[cite: 2].
  • Relative Motion: Governed by Newton's second law for a single fictitious particle of mass \(\mu\) in a central potential \(U(r)\):
    \[ \mu \ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -\nabla U(r) \]
    [cite: 2]
  • Angular Momentum Conservation: Total angular momentum in the CM frame is \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mu\dot{\mathbf{r}}\), which is conserved, confining motion to a fixed 2D plane[cite: 2].
  • Planar Polar Coordinates \((r, \phi)\):
    \[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\mu\left(\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\phi}^2\right) - U(r) \] \[ l = \mu r^2 \dot{\phi} = \text{constant} \quad (\phi\text{-equation}) \] \[ \mu\ddot{r} - \mu r\dot{\phi}^2 = -\frac{dU}{dr} \quad (r\text{-equation}) \]
    [cite: 2]

3. The Equivalent One-Dimensional Problem

  • Effective Potential Energy (\(U_{\text{eff}}\)): Eliminating \(\dot{\phi}\) using angular momentum \(l\) converts the radial motion to a 1D equivalent problem:
    \[ U_{\text{eff}}(r) = U(r) + U_{\text{cf}}(r) = U(r) + \frac{l^2}{2\mu r^2} \]
    where \(U_{\text{cf}}(r) = \frac{l^2}{2\mu r^2}\) is the centrifugal potential energy[cite: 2].
  • Conservation of Energy:
    \[ E = \frac{1}{2}\mu \dot{r}^2 + U_{\text{eff}}(r) \]
    [cite: 2]

4. Transformed Radial Equation and Kepler Orbits

  • Substitution Trick: Substituting \(u = \frac{1}{r}\) and transforming derivatives from \(t\) to \(\phi\) yields the transformed radial differential equation:
    \[ u''(\phi) = -u(\phi) - \frac{\mu}{l^2 u(\phi)^2} F\left(\frac{1}{u}\right) \]
    [cite: 2]
  • Inverse-Square Force Solution (\(F(r) = -\frac{\gamma}{r^2}\)):
    \[ r(\phi) = \frac{c}{1 + \epsilon \cos \phi} \]
    where scale parameter \(c = \frac{l^2}{\gamma \mu}\) and eccentricity \(\epsilon\) relates to total energy \(E\) by:
    \[ E = \frac{\gamma^2 \mu}{2l^2}(\epsilon^2 - 1) \]
    [cite: 2]

5. Classification of Kepler Orbits

Eccentricity (\(\epsilon\)) Energy (\(E\)) Orbit Shape
\(\epsilon = 0\) \(E < 0\) (Minimum) Circle
\(0 < \epsilon < 1\) \(E < 0\) Ellipse
\(\epsilon = 1\) \(E = 0\) Parabola
\(\epsilon > 1\) \(E > 0\) Hyperbola

6. Kepler's Third Law

  • For bounded elliptical orbits with semimajor axis \(a\), the orbital period \(\tau\) obeys:
    \[ \tau^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM_s} a^3 \]
    where \(M_s\) is the mass of the central attraction body (e.g., the Sun)[cite: 2].
